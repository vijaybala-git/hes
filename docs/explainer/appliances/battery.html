Battery: moving energy into the peak

A battery makes no energy and loses about a tenth of what passes through it. It earns its keep by timing: storing midday solar (or cheap off-peak power) and releasing it during the 4–9 pm peak, when electricity costs the most. Its value only shows up hour by hour.

Two modes, the cheaper one each month

Self-powered

Solar serves the home; surplus fills the battery; the battery covers any shortfall at any hour until empty. Best when there's plenty of solar and the goal is to use as little grid power as possible.

Cost-saving

Keeps a reserve just big enough for the peak and discharges only during it. If solar alone won't fill that reserve, it tops up from the grid before 4 pm, but only when that pays.

For every month the engine runs both and keeps whichever gives the lower bill, so the answer reflects a well-run battery. Try it in the hourly energy balance simulator.

When does charging from the grid pay?

Buying a kWh off-peak, storing it, and using it at peak returns only 0.89 kWh. So grid charging pays only when 0.89 × peak price > off-peak price. On PG&E E-TOU-C:

Season0.89 × peakoff-peakGrid charging

Tier-1 prices, 2026. A small price gap plus losses means winter grid charging doesn't pay on this plan; the battery then relies on solar.

What's modeled, what's simplified

  • Power and energy limits (5 kW in, 11.5 kW out, 13.5 kWh) and round-trip losses.
  • Steady state: each day ends at the charge it started with, so no free energy.
  • Both standard modes, cheaper chosen monthly.
  • No capacity fade over the years.
  • No backup-power value during outages.
  • Average day: no multi-day cloudy stretches; perfect foresight within the day.
  • No stand-alone battery (without solar) yet.
