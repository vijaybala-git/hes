Panel: can the wiring carry an electric home?

Many older California homes have a 100-amp panel. Swapping gas appliances for electric ones adds 240-volt circuits, and at some point the panel may need an upgrade, often a real cost. WhyWatt runs the same load calculation an electrician or permit office uses (NEC Article 220), year by year as your plan adds appliances. The panel uses no energy; it only adds an install cost if you plan the upgrade.

The rules, in brief

general = 3 VA × sq ft + 3,000 VA (two kitchen circuits) + 1,500 VA (laundry) appliances (nameplate volts × amps): heat pump = tons × 10 A · HPWH 15 A · dryer ≥ 5,000 VA range 8,000 VA (NEC 220.55) · EV charger × 1.25 (continuous load) optional (220.82): [first 10 kVA of general + non-HVAC appliances at 100%, the rest at 40%] + HVAC at 100% standard (220.42): [general: first 10 kVA at 100%, rest at 40%] + every appliance at 100% amps = VA ÷ 240

The optional method is what Bay Area permit offices accept for existing homes, and it's why a fully electric home can often fit a smaller service than people expect. The standard method always gives a higher number and is offered for comparison.

What's modeled, what's simplified

  • Code-based calculation, year by year as the plan adds appliances.
  • Both NEC methods, so you can see the difference.
  • Upgrade cost goes on the journey's CapEx timeline.
  • Advisory only: the check doesn't force an upgrade or change energy costs.
  • Load-management devices (smart panels, circuit sharing) that avoid an upgrade aren't modeled.
  • Not a substitute for an electrician's site check.
