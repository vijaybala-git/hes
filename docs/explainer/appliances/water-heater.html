Water heater: gas tank → heat-pump water heater

Hot water is usually a home's second-largest gas load. The energy needed is simple physics: how many gallons, times how many degrees each gallon is warmed from the water main to the tank setting, divided by how efficiently the heater does it.

The physics

Warming one gallon of water by 1°F takes 8.33 BTU. The water main is colder in winter, so each gallon needs more heating then. That monthly water temperature comes from the climate pipeline.

gas therms[m] = gallons/day × days[m] × 8.33 × (120°F − water_main[m]) × 0.00001 / UEF HPWH kWh[m] = gallons/day × days[m] × 8.33 × (120°F − water_main[m]) / 3,412 / (UEF × location)
Gallons per day
By bedrooms: 1 BR 30 · 2 BR 50 · 3 BR 65 · 4 BR 75 · 5 BR 85 (DOE / ENERGY STAR), or your own number.
UEF
Uniform Energy Factor: heat delivered to the water ÷ energy used. A gas tank loses heat up its flue; a heat pump moves ~3.5 units of heat per unit of electricity.
Location
A heat pump in a cold garage works harder: efficiency × 0.85 in unconditioned space, × 1.00 indoors.

The heat pump's monthly kWh is spread over the day with NREL's water_heating profile from ResStock's heat-pump-water-heater run: a morning shower peak and a smaller evening one. A heat-pump water heater can also be scheduled to heat ahead of the 4–9 pm peak; that isn't modeled yet.

What's modeled, what's simplified

  • Monthly water-main temperature for your climate zone, so winter hot water costs more.
  • Household size sets the gallons, and you can override it.
  • Garage installs pay an efficiency penalty.
  • Checked by tests: at the reference climate, the gas tank lands at ≈ 210 therms/yr and the HPWH at ≈ 1,050 kWh/yr (±5%).
  • Standby losses are inside UEF, not modeled separately.
  • Constant heat-pump efficiency: no switch to backup resistance heating on heavy-use or cold days.
  • No load shifting: a timer that heats before the peak would lower the bill.
