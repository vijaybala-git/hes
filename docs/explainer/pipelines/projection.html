Rate projection: how prices could grow over 20 years

A heat pump bought today runs for 15 years or more, so what matters is the price of electricity and gas over that whole period, not just today. WhyWatt doesn't make up growth rates: it follows the California Energy Commission's 2025 rate forecasts and offers three scenarios, so you can see how sensitive the answer is.

The pipeline

Offline, the CEC workbooks and the CPUC avoided-cost model are reduced to a growth curve per fuel and scenario. At run time the curve supplies only the shape: today's level always comes from your tariff. So a Moderate PG&E home and a Moderate SMUD home grow at the same pace from different starting prices.

rate[y] = current_rate × S[y] / S[anchor year] (anchor = the year the current rate is from)

The four curves

Each line is price relative to 2025 (1.0 = today's price). Electricity stays close to inflation: the CEC expects it to flatten, then rise. Gas is very different: see the note below.

Why the gas curves climb so steeply

The CEC gas forecasts model a shrinking gas system: as homes electrify, the cost of pipelines and safety work is spread over fewer and fewer customers. Conservative assumes the utility manages that decline; Stress assumes costs are recovered as today (a "death spiral"). All three are CEC planning cases, not WhyWatt estimates, and they sit above the independent E3 2020 high-electrification path. A review of the gas curve is open before a projection method becomes the app's default.

What's inside an electricity price

The build splits the retail price into the marginal cost of energy and delivery (from the CPUC avoided-cost model) and everything else: wires, wildfire mitigation, public programs. Nearly all the growth comes from the second part.

What's modeled, what's simplified

  • Grounded in the state's own forecasts, not a flat % guess.
  • Shape and level are separate, so every utility keeps its real starting price.
  • Scenarios, not one answer: the comparison can be rerun under each.
  • One growth shape for all of California: PG&E's CEC forecast stands in for every utility until others are built.
  • The app starts on WhyWatt Conservative. The older "My Utility" method (a fixed %/yr on the EIA rate: 4/4, 7/8 or 10/12 elec/gas) is still offered, and it is the engine's built-in fallback when no method is given.
  • Deterministic: no price spikes or year-to-year noise.
